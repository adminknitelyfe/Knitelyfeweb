Knite ID · Knite Exchange

Share your Knite, not your number.

Meeting someone from a marketplace listing, a dating app or a group chat usually starts with handing over a phone number to a stranger. Knite Exchange is being designed as the step in between — enough to know who you're dealing with, without giving away how to reach you everywhere else.

Knite Lyfe is in development. Everything on this page describes the intended product, not a shipped one.

The principle

Verify more. Reveal less.

Almost every identity check works by handing over more than the question required. Someone asks whether you're over 21 and you show a card carrying your address, your licence number and your date of birth.

Knite ID is designed the other way around. It confirms the specific things that matter for a real-world meeting, and shares the confirmation rather than the document behind it. The intent is that the other person learns you're real and roughly who you claim to be, and learns nothing else.

Prove the fact. Protect the data. That is the design goal, and it is the reason the exchange exists at all.

Knite Exchange

Send. Scan. Tap.

Three entry points into the same exchange. Which one you use depends on whether the other person is across a table or across the internet.

Send

Share a Knite Link through whatever you're already talking on — a marketplace thread, a dating app, a group chat, a message. Useful when you haven't met yet and don't want to swap numbers to find out whether you should.

Scan

Show your Knite QR and let the other person scan it. The code is intended to start an exchange rather than carry your details — nothing personal is meant to live inside the image itself.

Tap

Tap to Knite — bring two compatible phones together and the exchange starts. Meant to feel like swapping contacts, not running a background check. Tap depends on device support and is a planned capability, not a current one.

These are three doors into one system. What happens after you walk through is the same either way.

Verify is not connect

An exchange doesn't make you friends.

Scanning someone's Knite is not meant to create a permanent relationship. It answers a question, and then you decide what — if anything — happens next. A marketplace buyer may only ever need the first of these.

Option one

Verify

See the signals they chose to share, and nothing more. The exchange can end here, and often should.

Option two

Connect

Add each other as Knite connections, so a future exchange doesn't start from scratch.

Option three

Share

Hand over a way to reach you, once you've decided the person is worth reaching — a social handle or a username-based app, so it isn't your phone number by default.

Option four

Start a Safety Plan

Open a temporary session around the meeting itself — where, when, and who's expecting to hear from you.

Modes

Not every exchange should last forever.

A person you're about to sell a bike to does not need permanent access to anything. The intent is that the exchange itself can be scoped to the situation.

Standard

Standard Knite

A reusable exchange for ordinary social situations, where being reachable later is the point.

Time-limited

Temporary Knite

A link or code intended to stop working after a set window — long enough for the meeting, not longer.

Single use

One-Time Knite

Designed to become invalid once it has been used. Built for strangers and one-off transactions.

Mutual

Reciprocal Exchange

Both people agree first, and neither receives the other's information until both have accepted.

Trust should work both ways.

Reciprocal exchange matters more than it sounds. A one-directional check makes one person the subject of an investigation and the other the investigator, which is a strange way to begin. When both sides verify at once, it reads as what it is — two people establishing enough confidence to meet.

End to end

From online conversation to real-world connection.

This is where Knite ID stops being a credential and becomes part of the safety system. The exchange establishes who; the plan handles what happens next.

1 · Meet online

A listing, a match, a group chat, a gaming server. The conversation starts somewhere you'd rather not hand over a phone number.

2 · Send your Knite

A link goes through the platform you're already using. No new account required for them to look at it.

3 · Verify each other

Both sides see the signals the other chose to share. Reciprocal by default, so nobody is being audited.

4 · Choose what to share

Maybe nothing beyond the verification. Maybe a first name and a photo. Maybe a number, once there's a reason.

5 · Start a Safety Plan

For a meeting that warrants it: where, when, how long, and which trusted contact is expecting to hear from you.

6 · Meet, then close the plan

You confirm you're done. The session ends and sharing stops with it.

Verification does not mean safe.

This is the most important sentence on the page, and we would rather it were the one you remember.

  • What verification can help establish: that the person is real, that certain identity attributes have been confirmed, and that they are not entirely anonymous
  • What it cannot establish: character, intentions, future behaviour, or criminal history
  • A verified person can still be someone you should not meet. A Knite is a piece of context, not a character reference
  • Knite Lyfe is not an emergency service and cannot guarantee anyone's safety

Verification adds context. It does not replace judgement.

How we're approaching privacy

Don't reveal what you don't need to.

A privacy promise built on stopping screenshots is not a privacy promise. If information has been handed over, the question of what happens to it next is already out of your control.

So the approach is to limit what leaves in the first place. The aim is that an exchange carries the outcome of a check rather than the material behind it — a confirmation that something is true, not the document that proved it.

Formal privacy documentation, data-retention practice and independent security review are still being completed, and we will publish specifics when they are real rather than aspirational.

Somewhere to talk that isn't your number

Knite Lyfe is not going to be another inbox. There are enough of those, and a safety app is a strange place to keep a conversation.

What an exchange can carry instead is a way to reach you that isn't tied to your phone. A handle on a platform that identifies people by username rather than number means the other person can message you about the pickup time — and you can block them, or simply stop replying, without having handed over something attached to your identity, your bank, your two-factor codes and every account you own.

A phone number is permanent and difficult to take back. A username is neither. That difference is the whole point.

Early access

Verify once. Use your Knite again.

A Knite ID is intended to be set up once and reused — every marketplace meetup, every first meeting, every time someone reasonably wants to know you're real. We're building it now and would rather hear from people who'd actually use it.
